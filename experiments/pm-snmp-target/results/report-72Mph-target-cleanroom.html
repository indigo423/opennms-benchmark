The measurement window was 900 seconds — exactly three collection cycles — and produced 19,832,108 samples across 3,803 monitored nodes with no read warnings and complete node coverage. A second identical run returned 19,832,106 samples, two apart in nearly twenty million. Rate is computed against the wall-clock window, not against the span of message timestamps.

At that rate every component still had headroom. Core ran at 0.93 load per core, the database at 0.11, the Minion at 0.11 and the load generator at 0.09. Nothing in the stack was saturated.

Read this before using the numbers

These are minimum requirements, not a sizing target

Every figure here was produced under conditions that are close to the best case an OpenNMS deployment can encounter. The simulated SNMP agents answer from an in-memory table in microseconds, never time out, never drop a packet and never rate-limit. All six virtual machines sit on one physical host connected by in-kernel Linux bridges, so round-trip latency is sub-millisecond with no jitter and no loss.

Collectd threads spend their time blocked on SNMP round trips. When an agent answers in 40 ms instead of 40 µs, the same thread count sustains a small fraction of the devices. Treat this configuration as the floor below which the workload will not fit at all, and expect a real estate of the same size to need materially more.

Why the Minion needs 4 vCPU, not 2

The Minion executes every SNMP walk that Collectd's threads block on, so it is the RPC executor for the whole workload. It began the campaign at 2 vCPU and was raised to 4 on 27 August at 22:25 CEST, with the fleet held at 2,154 collectable services on both sides of the change, which makes the two halves of that evening directly comparable.

As a percentage the resize looks unnecessary, and that is the trap. CPU busy fell from 26.1% of two vCPU to 12.0% of four, which reads like a machine with twice the headroom. But a percentage divides by exactly the quantity the resize changed, so identical work halves on the chart without anything having improved. Measured in cores, which does not move with the core count, the Minion drew 0.50 of a core before and 0.44 after — the same half-core of work, and throughput did not move either.

The absolute peaks went the other way. Taking the highest value in each 10-minute bucket, CPU demand rose across the resize: a median of 0.85 cores before against 0.96 after, and a worst case of 1.44 cores against 1.81. The work was always there. At 2 vCPU it could not all be dispatched at once, so it queued instead of running, and the percentage view showed peaks halving at the moment the machine actually began doing more per burst.

The run queue is the measure that does not move with the core count. Load average counts processes ready to run, so it can be read straight against the number of cores — which is why the figure below plots the two together. At 2 vCPU the one-minute load stood above two in 21% of samples and reached 6.30, three runnable processes deep per core. At 4 vCPU it crossed the four-core line once in 248 samples. Peak run-queue depth itself barely changed, 31 processes before and 35 after: the extra cores did not make the Minion do more, they gave the burst somewhere to land.

The fleet on 27 August was also little more than half its final size, 2,154 collectable services against 3,805 in the measured window. At the final size the Minion on 4 vCPU averages 0.49 of a core and peaks at 1.41 in a 10-minute bucket. Two cores would have to absorb that same peak with half the places to put it — the position the 27 August evening had already shown queues.

That one policy is what the whole benchmark's density rests on. Cutting the detectors to two is the cheaper decision of the pair: at thousands of homogeneous simulated devices the stock detector suite dominates import time while establishing nothing that is not already known about the generator.

How the fleet was grown, and what the three counts mean

This run added to a fleet that was already provisioned rather than resetting it, because a reset discards the nodes and pays to rediscover them. Seven batches — six of 250 and one of 150, 1,650 devices — went onto a standing fleet of about 2,150, one /24 per batch on third octets 11 to 17, the range 0 to 10 having been consumed by the earlier sweep.

Only one of a device node's three services is collected. SNMP is the collectable one; ICMP is polled by Pollerd and never reaches Collectd, and gNMI-Telemetry exists to activate the OpenConfigConnector in telemetryd. So the fleet contributes exactly one collectable service per device, and every metric counted in this report comes through that one service.

The remaining four are the same four at every fleet size. At the 2,150-device fleet the counter read 2,154; at 3,801 it read 3,805. Those four sit on the two extra nodes — the Core's own localhost node and the Minion — which are not part of this requisition, come from the OpenNMS package's own configuration, and are collected over JMX rather than SNMP. This deployment does not template collectd-configuration.xml; the single change it makes to the packaged file is the thread count.

What that does and does not settle. It settles the node arithmetic: the per-device figures below divide by 3,801 SNMP services and are already clean of the two JMX nodes. It does not settle their share of the samples, and the two are not interchangeable — a JVM collection returns far more attributes per service than an interface walk does, so four services out of 3,805 is not four samples in 3,805. Separating the JMX contribution from the metric total needs the run's own sidecar, not the configuration.

A metric here is one numeric attribute on one resource in one collection cycle. Message count is not a proxy for it: a single Kafka CollectionSet carries many resources, each with many attributes, and the producer may split one set across several messages.

Interface counters dominate, and the twelve are exactly enumerable. mib2-X-interfaces contributes ifHCInOctets and ifHCOutOctets; mib2-X-interfaces-pkts adds the six 64-bit packet counters, unicast, multicast and broadcast in each direction; mib2-interface-errors adds discards and errors in each direction. Note what is not among them: ifName and ifHighSpeed are declared as strings, so interface speed is stored as an attribute but never counted as a sample.

The node-level side is thinner than it looks. Two systemDef entries match this device's sysObjectID — Cisco Routers on the mask .1.3.6.1.4.1.9.1. and Enterprise on .1.3.6.1.4.1. — and between them they enable eighteen groups. Only three of those target a single per-node resource: mib2-tcp, cisco-router and cisco-memory. The other fifteen are per-interface or indexed by a table this device does not populate: frame relay, DOCSIS channels, BGP peers, RTT monitor probes, environmental sensors. On this fleet only mib2-tcp's ten attributes return, which is why 144 × 12 + 10 reproduces the measured 1,738 exactly.

One simulator defect is worth knowing about. In the CRS-X profile the OID for freeMem returns the device name as a string where the MIB requires a gauge, so OpenNMS logs a conversion error on every poll of every device and never collects that attribute. At this fleet size that was roughly 14 errors per second. Reported upstream as nl6#515; it depresses the per-device count slightly and costs real CPU in logging.

Configuration

What has to change from stock

A default installation does not reach this volume. The changes below are what separated a system that could not complete a collection cycle from one that had headroom to spare.

PoweredBy 2026 is the same codebase as OpenNMS Horizon 36.0.3 in a different distribution, so the upstream name is used throughout wherever it is what a reader will actually see: opennms.conf, opennms-datasources.xml and the org.opennms.* properties below are what the running system reads, and the daemons keep their upstream names. Every figure in this report transfers between the two distributions without adjustment.

Collectd thread pool collectd-configuration.xml

  • threads: 50 → 100. The thread count bounds how many collections can be in flight. With 2,154 collectable services on a 300 s interval, 50 threads leaves under 7 s per node, which a 144-interface walk does not meet: the cycle finished at 78% completion with 2,104 services queued.
  • Threads block on SNMP round trips executed by the Minion, not on Core's CPU, so a value well above the vCPU count is correct rather than a mistake. 100 is a safe value, not a measured optimum — at 200 the pool thrashed and starved Core's own RPC response consumer, taking the Minion's services down while the Minion itself sat idle.

Thresholding, removed collectd-configuration.xml, thresholds.xml, threshd-configuration.xml

  • Thresholding runs inside Collectd's persistence path and issues metadata-scope database calls per evaluation. With it enabled an earlier smoke system collapsed at 250 devices; with it disabled the same hardware collected 1,500 devices at 97% coverage.
  • Set thresholding-enabled=false on every service, empty the threshold definitions, and remove the threshold packages. Verify by content, not by the play succeeding.

Kafka Producer, exclusive forwarding org.opennms.features.kafka.producer.cfg

  • forward.metrics = true with metricTopic = metrics. Metrics leave Core only through Kafka; the OSGi context installs EmptyResourceStorageDao, so there is no local time-series store and the OpenNMS UI shows no graphs by design.
  • The Kafka topic is the measurement point. If the producer feature is missing, Collectd blocks rather than failing, and the deployment collects nothing while reporting success.

Database connection pool opennms.conf

  • OPENNMS_DATABASE_CONNECTION_MAXPOOL and MAXSIZE: 50 → 250, MINPOOL 25. At the stock 50 the pool sat pinned at 50 active with up to 127 threads queued on every collection cycle, which is Collectd blocking on connection acquisition rather than on the database.
  • These must be set as environment variables. opennms-datasources.xml reads them as ${env:OPENNMS_DATABASE_CONNECTION_MAXSIZE|50}, so a value set anywhere else is silently ignored and the fallback stays in force.

PostgreSQL postgresql.conf / conf.d

  • Packaged defaults are sized for a far smaller machine and do not scale with the VM: 128 MB shared_buffers and 100 max_connections regardless of available memory.
  • Applied, following pgtune's web profile for 8 vCPU and 16 GB: max_connections 400, shared_buffers 3.9GB, effective_cache_size 11.7GB, work_mem 10MB, maintenance_work_mem 1GB, max_parallel_workers 8, random_page_cost 1.1, effective_io_concurrency 200.
  • max_connections must exceed the sum of every pool. OpenNMS opens three: the main pool plus two fixed at 50. A pool larger than the server allows fails at connect time and appears as daemons blocking, not as a database error.

Kafka broker server.properties

  • log.segment.bytes: 1 GiB → 128 MiB. Retention deletes only closed segments, so 1 GiB segments let each partition hold roughly 1.5 GiB before anything becomes eligible. A 1,500-device stream filled a 19 GB disk in about an hour and took the broker offline mid-run.
  • log.retention.bytes = 512MiB per partition and log.retention.hours = 24. Retention bytes are per partition, so the real ceiling is bytes × partitions × topics.

JVM opennms.conf

  • JAVA_HEAP_SIZE = 8192, unchanged throughout. Core used 10.3 GB of its 16 GB with 5.7 GB still available while saturating its cores, so heap was never the constraint and does not need to scale with vCPU.

The database is not the constraint under exclusive Kafka forwarding. Metrics bypass PostgreSQL entirely, so across the window the database VM stayed under 13% busy at 0.22 load per core — the least loaded component in the lab apart from the broker. Its 8 vCPU is sized for provisioning and node-scan bursts, not for steady-state collection, and the tuning above matters because the packaged defaults would throttle those bursts, not because collection is heavy on it.

Both ends of every bridge agree, which is what makes the interface mapping a measurement rather than an assumption. The lab does not label its NICs by segment, so which enp6s* belongs to which bridge was established by matching throughput across the wire: the Minion transmits 3.98 Mbit/s on enp6s20 and the load generator receives 3.98 Mbit/s on enp6s19, to the second decimal. The Kafka bridge closes the same way in both directions — the broker receives 51.44 Mbit/s against 51.39 produced by Core and the Minion together, and transmits 37.14 against 37.11 consumed by them — a residual of 0.05 Mbit/s, which is the monitoring VM's share of a segment it is also attached to.

The whole SNMP workload is 11 Mbit/s. Collecting 79.3 million metrics an hour from 3,801 devices costs less on the wire than a single video stream, and the largest flow in the lab is not the collection at all: it is the Minion returning RPC responses to Kafka at 34.3 Mbit/s, roughly three times what the SNMP itself weighs. Of Core's 17.12 Mbit/s onto the bridge, the RPC requests the Minion receives account for 2.07, leaving roughly 15 Mbit/s as the metrics topic itself — about 85 bytes per sample at 22,036 samples per second. Network was never a constraint here, but the ratio is the part worth carrying away: the transport costs more than the telemetry.

The constraint you will meet first

Collection itself was never the limiting factor. Sampling the JVM repeatedly at this fleet size found none of the 100 Collectd threads runnable; the pool sits idle between cycles and throughput is bounded by what the fleet emits.

Node scanning is a different story. Provisioning 1,650 new nodes through a single Minion spent several minutes retrying before SNMP interfaces persisted. Scan throughput, not collection throughput, is the practical ceiling on a single Minion, and it is the first thing to distribute when the estate grows.

Method

How the rate was measured

A consumer reads the Kafka metrics topic between two recorded offset bounds, decodes each CollectionSet and counts numeric attributes. Two properties of that measurement matter enough to state:

  • Rate is computed against the wall-clock window, not against the span between the first and last message timestamp. Dividing by the span inflates the result whenever collection does not fill the window — one such reading suggested a 40% throughput gain that was really a backlog drain of exactly 1.5 cycles.
  • The window must be an integer multiple of the collection interval. Collection arrives in bursts, with a measured peak-to-mean sample rate of 2.8 rising past 4.2 after a restart. A window of 2.2 cycles captures two or three whole bursts depending on its phase.
  • This is not a theoretical concern. The same fleet measured over a 660 s window read 24,652 samples/s, and over a 900 s window read 22,036. The shorter window over-reported by 11.9%. Three earlier 660 s runs agreed with each other to within 4%, which looked like precision and was in fact a shared phase bias: repeatability at the wrong window size confirms nothing.

Coverage — timestamp span divided by window — is bounded on both sides. Below the floor the window did not fill; above the ceiling it drained backlog. Either way the rate is not a sustained one.

Where the graphs come from. Every figure in this report is rendered from the lab's own monitoring: Prometheus on mon-benchmark-01, read through the same queries that drive the Grafana dashboards Node Exporter Full, Prometheus: Java Virtual Machine and Prometheus: OpenNMS Core Internals. The observation window is 2026-08-28 14:00 to 2026-08-30 23:00 CEST, 57 hours at 10-minute resolution with no scrape gaps. One figure is deliberately outside it: the Minion sizing chart covers 27 August, because the resize it documents happened before the measured run began and cannot be seen from inside the window. Gauges that spike faster than the sampling grid (pool occupancy, queue depth) are plotted as per-bucket peaks via max_over_time, because instantaneous samples alias the bursts to zero.
